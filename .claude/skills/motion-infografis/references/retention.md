# Retention pacing (why the opening is dense)

Short-form viewers decide in the first seconds whether to keep watching, so the motion plan is front-loaded: the
opening gets the strongest visuals and the fastest rhythm, and the body keeps changing often enough that the frame
never goes stale. These rules come from platform research and industry retention data (sources below), plus the
user's own note: in the first cut of "banyak jasa chaos", the second graphic only arrived at 0:15, after 12 s of plain
footage, which is exactly where viewers drop.

## What the data says
- **The first 3 seconds carry most of the value.** Meta: 65 % of people who watch the first 3 s of a video keep watching
  for at least 10 s, and 45 % for 30 s. With Nielsen, Meta found up to 47 % of a video campaign's value was delivered in
  the first 3 s and up to 74 % in the first 10 s.
- **Say the point early.** TikTok for Business: over 63 % of the videos with the highest click-through rate put their key
  message or product in the first 3 s, and TikTok recommends introducing the proposition in the first 3 s.
- **The swipe decision is even faster.** Industry analyses put the median scroll-stop at about 1.5–2 s, and report that
  roughly 7 in 10 viewers decide to stay or swipe within 3 s. Treat 0–1.5 s as the real hook window.
- **Stale frames lose people.** Editors and retention tools report that after about 12–15 s of the same angle, distance
  and background, a meaningful share of viewers scroll; a pattern interrupt (cut, zoom, text, sound, layout change)
  resets that clock. High-energy edits change something every 2–3 s, retention-first edits every 3–5 s.
- **Do not use the "8-second attention span / goldfish" claim.** It traces to an unsourced infographic, not to research.
  Attention is earned by novelty and relevance, second by second, not fixed at a number.

## Rules this skill follows
| window | rule | how |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0–0.5 s | something designed is already on screen | hook `title` (often `upper: true`), a `zoom` punch, or a `kinetic` hook word |
| by 3 s | the key message is readable on screen, in the speaker's words | hook title carries the claim, not a logo or a greeting |
| 0–10 s | at least 3 visual events, no more than ~3 s between them | title → zoom punch → callout / split / kinetic word; each is a pattern interrupt |
| second beat | starts no later than ~1 s after the first ends (and before 5 s) | never 3 s of hook then 12 s of plain footage |
| 10 s → end | no stretch longer than ~8 s without *some* change | light interrupts count: `zoom` punch, `callout`, `annotate` mark, a layout switch |
| around 40–60 % | a re-hook: the strongest visual of the body | `kinetic` interlude, `stat`, `split` proof, a `transition` into a new section |
| last 3–4 s | CTA holds to the final frame | no fade-out on the last frame, so the loop restarts cleanly |

Clean footage is still the goal in the body (30–50 % of the runtime with no card over the speaker), because the face is
why people watch. Light interrupts (`zoom`, a small `callout`, an `annotate` stroke) do not cover the face and do not
count against clean footage. The opening 10 s is the exception: keep plain footage there under ~30 %.

Sound-off viewing is common, so the hook has to land visually too: the claim must be readable, not only spoken.

## Check before showing the plan
Mark the timeline in 5-second buckets. Every bucket needs at least one visual event, and the 0–5 s and 5–10 s buckets
need at least two each. If a bucket is empty, add a light interrupt on the key word spoken in it.
